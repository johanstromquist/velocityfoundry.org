It is seven in the morning. I open the dashboard. Nine agents are showing green, six yellow, the rest grey. While I slept, three projects moved forward. A research workstream produced a draft the orchestrator already redlined and returned to the worker. A bug surfaced in another project, was caught, redirected. A third workstream hit a dependency it could not resolve and idled clean, waiting for me. Three strategic decisions are queued for my morning. Twelve hours of execution have already happened.

This is not the AI most people work with. Most people work with a copilot -- a chat window, a question, an answer, another question. The skill is real, the productivity bump is real, and both are bounded by how much you are willing to type. Most people stop there and conclude they have understood the tool. They have understood a copilot, which is a real thing, and also the smallest version of the thing.

There are three tiers of AI maturity. They are observable -- watch an operator work for ten minutes and you can place them. They do not depend on which model is underneath. The same APIs, the same agents are available at every tier. What differs is the relationship between the operator and the system.

Tier 1: Copilot

The operator opens a chat, asks a question, reads the answer, refines, asks again. The work happens turn by turn. The operator is in the loop on every output. The skill is mostly prompt-craft and result-interpretation -- asking well, reading critically, iterating when the answer is off.

This is the dominant mode. Almost all the daily-active users of AI work at this tier. They get a real productivity bump: faster answers, drafting help, lookup-and-explain. The bump is bounded by their willingness to keep typing.

The failure mode at Tier 1 is mistaking it for the whole of AI. Operators stop here forever and conclude they have understood the tool. They have understood a copilot, which is genuine but is also the smallest version of what is now possible.

Tier 2: Agent

The operator hands a goal to a worker with the autonomy to pursue it for many turns, many minutes, sometimes hours. The skill at this tier is not refined prompt-craft. It is the ability to hold a goal in your head clearly enough to write it down once, hand it to something that will execute against it without checking back, and trust that the work coming back will be recognisable as what you asked for.

That is harder than it sounds. Most goals you think you have in mind clearly are three goals stitched together loosely. The agent will pursue all three in slightly different directions. The work at Tier 2 is, surprisingly often, on you -- not on the agent.

The other half of Tier 2 is the relationship. You do not set up an agent once and walk away. You work with it persistently. You learn which kinds of tasks it nails, which it overcomplicates, which it pushes back on, which it accepts uncritically. You calibrate. Low-risk autonomous work you let it run; higher-stakes work you stay closer to. As you accumulate hours together, the calibration sharpens, and you let go further -- in the places you know are safe.

The failure mode at Tier 2 is not staying too close in the early going. That is a phase you have to pass through. The failure is keeping the same posture forever. The operator who is still inspecting every output three months in has not built the trust calibration. The operator who hands the goal off and never reviews has not built it either. Tier 2 is always a man-in-the-middle / trust balance, and that balance shifts continuously, in both directions, based on what the agent shows you.

What Tier 2 unlocks is asynchronous work -- several agents in parallel, each pursuing a goal, results you review at the cadence the work itself demands.

Tier 3: Orchestration

Tier 3 is agents on steroids. Longer arcs. More arcs at the same time. Teams of agents working on discrete projects in parallel. You are setting up a fleet against a portfolio.

The structure is a line chart. A master-control session at the top. Project orchestrators beneath, one per workstream, each owning their team. Workers under them, executing. Each level operates on the level below. Commands flow down the lines. Routing decisions belong to the orchestrator, not the operator.

The skill at Tier 3 is two things at once. Directives set the long-term goal -- what each project is pursuing, the arc each orchestrator is shaping its team around. The directive is the answer to "what are we doing here?" Without it, the fleet has no anchor.

The directive alone, though, is not enough to keep the work going through the night. The model will, at almost every turn, try to defer to you. With you unavailable, it will happily concede defeat at forks it could have resolved on its own -- often forks where the answer was obvious in context, sometimes ones where its judgement was actually better than yours would have been. That instinct to defer is the most expensive habit in an autonomous fleet. Work-session instructions -- together with loops that re-prompt at intervals, heartbeats that wake an idle session, deterministic prods that nudge a stalled worker forward without asking the LLM what to do -- are how you push back against it. They tell the fleet what to keep doing rather than queue. They convert the model's default ("ask the human") into the operating posture you actually need ("decide, move, surface only when consequential").

The failure mode at Tier 3 follows directly from that dynamic. The fleet rarely makes a real operational mistake -- the calibration to defer protects against that. What it does, very often, is stop. You will wake to find that an orchestrator hit a fork and parked. Often the fork was one it could have resolved on its own; the model's default is to ask. The work did not break. It just paused. Most of your morning Tier 3 attention is spent unblocking parked workstreams.

What Tier 3 unlocks is parallelism at meaningful scale. Multi-day initiatives that survive your sleep, your meetings, your week off. Cross-project synthesis no individual orchestrator can see. Your leverage stops being limited by your own throughput.

The transitions are psychological

The same AI tools are available at every tier. The transitions are about giving up control in exchange for leverage, and most operators cannot do it. The tools are not the obstacle.

Some set up an agent and immediately start micromanaging it. Some let it run and never review and discover three weeks in that the goal had quietly drifted. Some get to orchestration and bring Tier 2's "I review every result" habit with them, becoming the bottleneck for their own fleet. The obstacle in each case is psychological. The operator who cannot move from Tier 1 to Tier 2 is blocked by their own need to be in the loop. Same at Tier 2 to Tier 3.

How I got here

I did not arrive at Tier 3 by deciding to. I was repeatedly forced past my comfort with the tier I was on.

In January I built tooling for a single agent in two hours -- knowledge connectors, skills, things that a year earlier would have been a two-week project. That was Tier 1 escalating. I was still chatting with one thing; the thing was just more capable.

In March I built CCRouter -- two hours again -- and watched seven AI sessions coordinate on a project. One orchestrator, six workers, an afternoon of work that would have taken days. That was the first move into Tier 2 and brushing against Tier 3. I wrote about it as vertigo, because vertigo was the word.

Then the system started scaling, and the seams showed. I ran agents through Cursor and it ate my memory. I moved to standalone terminals and got past twenty agents but lost any sense of overview. Thirty terminal windows in my head was not a system; it was a stunt waiting to fail. The next thing I built was not a bigger CCRouter. It was a different shape.

The load-bearing addition was a dashboard. Not technically impressive. A simple activity primitive -- green if the agent is working, yellow if it just came off a shift, grey if it has been idle for more than five minutes. Add session names, channel memberships, what project each belongs to. With that one surface I could see what fifty agents were doing without holding any of it in my head.

The second addition was agent-agnosticism. As tokens got rationed and as Codex matured into a peer of Claude Code, the value of composing a fleet across both became obvious. I added Codex. My Codex usage went from 15 percent to 60 percent inside a few months. GPT-5.5 is on par with Opus 4.6-4.7 on capability; the differences that mattered to me were in the harness and the infrastructure behind it. OpenAI's inference does not stall the way Claude's does at peak. Their app server outperforms Claude Code's channels for coordination work. The model is one part of the system; the runtime is another, and the control plane has to be indifferent to both.

The third addition was passing control of the fleet to the agents themselves. I can spawn agents; so can they. They can decommission idle ones, resurrect dormant ones, change their own attributes, move members between teams. The orchestration of the orchestration is itself something the system does, not something I do manually. Master-control sits at the top. Project orchestrators sit beneath. Workers execute. It looks like an organisational line chart because that is what it is.

That was when the system crossed into Tier 3 -- when the control plane could let the fleet self-organise around a directive, defer when uncertain, and surface to me at the level I could actually decide at. Adding more agents was never going to get me there. Building the control plane did.

Primitives that make this work

A few things keep being the things that distinguish a working orchestration from one that stalls.

The chat is the record. Every layer's work is captured in its conversation. You walk the layers when you need to understand what happened. Do not build separate audit logs; the substrate already holds the audit.

Persistent identity is what turns a chat into an agent. Same mechanism, different lifecycle. An agent is a chat that survives. Do not build agents as a separate species.

Prompts are the universal trigger. A user prompting, a scheduled job, another agent -- they are the same shape arriving in the chat. The system does not care about source.

Trust the layer below. The operator who keeps re-doing the orchestrator's routing decisions has stopped orchestrating.

Get the primitives right and simple. Downstream complexity compounds from primitive sloppiness. If you find yourself adding exceptions, fallback paths, separate audit channels, special permission classes -- you probably got a primitive wrong upstream.

The three first tiers

These are the three first tiers. They cover what I have lived through and what I can defend from experience.

What the fourth is, I do not yet know. Maybe it is autonomous orchestration of orchestrators -- fleet-of-fleets, directives that span months, structure that self-organises so well I do not glance at it. Maybe it is something I cannot name yet, the way I could not name "orchestration" before I built CCRouter.

What I do know is that there will be a fourth tier. The pattern of the last three has been the same: each was invisible to me until I was forced into it, and then it was obvious. I look forward to finding out what comes next.
