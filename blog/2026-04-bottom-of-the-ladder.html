In 2018, a translator working between English and a major European language could expect to send back several finished commercial contracts in a working week. By 2023 that work was largely gone. The contracts were still being translated -- companies still had foreign suppliers and foreign markets -- but the translation was being done by software, with a human brought in only to check that the software had not tripped over a clause. The rate per page for the checking work was a fraction of the rate for the writing work that had preceded it.

That translator had not lost the ability to translate. What had changed was that the entry layer of their profession -- the bulk commercial work that used to underwrite a career -- had been pulled out from under them. Junior translators do not enter the field through that work any more, because that work is not there to do.

The same pattern, elsewhere

The pattern is visible wherever AI tools have arrived. Walk into a corporate legal practice and ask what a paralegal or first-year associate did five years ago. Document review. Contract drafting from templates. Due diligence on routine transactions. Most of that has shifted to a tool, with a human checking the output -- and a lot of firms have decided they need fewer of those checkers, or none.

In software, the junior developer used to write the routine modules: form validation, the boilerplate around an API, the glue code between services. Most of that is now drafted by a coding assistant. Stack Overflow's traffic, after fifteen years of compounding growth, fell off a cliff in early 2023 and has not recovered. Early-career hiring in software has dropped sharply since AI tools arrived. Older workers in the same roles have not seen the same effect.

The pattern is not subtle. The bottom of the role has collapsed. The work has not disappeared -- the contracts still get translated, the documents still get reviewed, the boilerplate still gets written -- but the human paid to do it is gone, or going.

A labour market being rebuilt

A labour market gets rebuilt every year. People retire, fields shift, demand moves, technology changes the cost of doing things. What is happening now is not categorically different from that. What is different is the speed and the direction: the fastest commoditisation of a class of work in living memory, hitting the bottom of dozens of professions at once.

The cheap response is to stop hiring at the entry level. The AI is cheaper than a junior. Big-law cohorts are smaller. Translation agencies have cut their junior rosters. The visible result is a market that increasingly has experts and tools, with nothing structural between them. The labour market bleeds from the bottom up.

There is another response. It treats AI as an extension of the workforce -- which it already is, for everyone using it -- and asks the question that follows: with AI carrying the throughput, what can you put a junior person in front of?

The recent answer is striking. In April this year, Liam Price -- 23 years old, with no advanced mathematics training -- prompted GPT-5.4 Pro with a long-open Erdős conjecture about primitive sets of integers. The model's response -- a single prompt, no follow-ups -- contained a proof that two senior mathematicians, Terence Tao and Jared Duker Lichtman, then refined and formalised in Lean. Tao's read on what had happened: earlier mathematicians had collectively taken "a slight wrong turn at move one" and stayed inside it for decades. The AI, free of the field's habits, tried a different opening move, and the proof came out. The raw output was poor -- it took experts to sift through it and recognise what was there. The insight was real, and it has applications beyond the one problem.

That is an extreme case. Most of what gets done in firms is not Erdős problems. But the structure is the same: an operator working far above their own ceiling, AI generating output the operator alone could not have produced, and senior review converting raw output into something usable. The Price case is an accident. The same structure can be designed.

A different design

At SSI, where I work, the design we are trying to build looks like this. Junior associates, and professionals from adjacent fields who can be brought in because the AI rail guard makes the move feasible, come in as first drafters working alongside AI. Their job is not to produce the throughput from scratch. The AI does that. The drafter's job is to take the AI's first round, hold it against the standard the work actually requires, mark up what is wrong with it, and send it forward. A senior expert then reviews what the drafter has produced -- both the AI's draft and the drafter's redlines -- and provides another round of review and coaching. The redlines from the senior do two things. The first is the deliverable: the work leaves at the standard the client paid for. The second is the drafter: they see, in real time, the difference between what they noticed and what an experienced practitioner noticed, on their own work. Both happen inside the same pass, on work the client commissioned. AI and coaching by design -- the mentoring is the rhythm of the work, not a margin cost set aside from it.

The point is to redefine what "entry level" means. The old version was unsupervised throughput, learned by repetition. The new version is supervised work-at-a-higher-level, learned by coached review. The drafter is operating at a level of sophistication that was not available to a first-year practitioner a decade ago, because the AI is doing the parts that used to fill the first three years.

This is early. We are not claiming it works at scale. It is the design we are trying to build, not a system we are sitting on top of. But the structure of it -- AI handling the throughput, supervised work as the entry layer, coaching as the engine of progression -- is what makes it possible to keep hiring at the bottom of the ladder while the bottom of the ladder is being rebuilt.

None of this falls out of the spreadsheet

The spreadsheet says: don't hire the juniors; the AI is cheaper. A lot of firms are running that spreadsheet right now. What the spreadsheet is missing is that the coaching does not have to come out of margin. If the junior is producing real client drafts, with AI as rail guard, and the senior reviews them as part of the normal billable pass, the mentoring is folded into work the client was paying for anyway. The client gets the deliverable faster. The firm gets a developing practitioner. None of it costs the firm time outside the work.

What the spreadsheet has trouble with is the premise: that an entry-level person, equipped this way, can produce senior-level draft work. Allow the premise, and the redesign is the cheaper option in the short term, not only the durable one in the long term. The cheap option wins by default because the spreadsheet does not imagine the alternative.

The labour market is being rebuilt right now, in real time, at every firm choosing whether to hire at the entry level this year. Most are taking what looks like the rational efficiency. Some are doing something else. The other path is open. It does not require any new technology. It requires the imagination to point existing people at new work, supported by AI, supervised by experience. The bottom of the ladder is still there. It is just shaped differently.
