In 1888, George Eastman sold a camera loaded with a roll of 100 exposures. When the roll was finished, the customer sent the whole camera back to Rochester, where Kodak processed the film and reloaded it. The promise fitted on one line: “You press the button, we do the rest.” Nobody had to learn to load film or develop it.

Agents make a similar offer now. Describe the result, and the agent does most of the making.

But the person directing the agent still has to choose what is worth making, and judge whether the result is right. Those two jobs do not pass to the agent along with the implementation.

When making gets cheaper, choosing matters more

Twelve years after the first Kodak, the one-dollar Brownie brought the same deal to far more people. Amateurs formed clubs and published magazines. Family albums filled with ordinary scenes, often captioned, with jokes and collages. The camera changed who could take photographs, and how many they took. Photography stayed a skilled practice all the same.

What changed was the weight of selection. In 1902 Alfred Stieglitz and other photographers formed the Photo-Secession, arguing that snapshots lacked the skill and judgement that art required. Later artists took up the snapshot’s immediacy and uneven framing. Walker Evans used ordinary photographs as material for his own work. Robert Frank and William Klein drew on snapshot conventions in theirs. Pictures taken for family albums became records that other people could read.

The camera did not decide what went into a family album or an art series. It made taking the picture easier. Someone still had to point it, decide when to press the button and pick which frames to keep. Once anyone could make an exposure, selection became a larger part of what gave a set of photographs its meaning.

Choosing is the first job that stays with you when an agent does the making. The agent can offer more screens and more features. It cannot know which of them serve the person who will use the result unless someone has set that purpose.

Supervising takes practice

A study of airline pilots looked at what changed when cockpit automation took over more of the flying. Sixteen pilots flew routine and non-routine scenarios in a Boeing 747-400 simulator. Their basic instrument scanning and manual control were mostly intact. Other tasks gave them more frequent and more serious trouble: keeping track of the aircraft’s position without a map display, choosing the next navigation step, and recognising failed instruments. The pilots who supervised the automation more actively did better on those tasks.

Hand-flying is the visible part of the job. Knowing where the aircraft is and what should happen next is work too. When automation handles routine control, the pilot still has to watch the system and make sense of what it does. That ability needs practice. It does not appear on its own once the pilot lets go of the controls.

Directing an agent splits the same way. The agent edits files and runs tests. You decide what the change is for, whether the result fits that purpose, and what to do when the evidence and the report disagree. You make those judgements while the work is going on. They are not a signature added at the end.

Speed does not tell you whether it helped

In one controlled experiment, programmers using Copilot finished a standardised JavaScript server task 55.8% faster. Finished meant a first commit that passed twelve tests. For that task the result is real, and it is a good reason to want an agent’s help with bounded work.

METR’s study of experienced open-source developers came out the other way. Working in repositories they already knew, the developers were 19% slower with early-2025 AI tools. Before they started, they expected to be 24% faster. Afterwards, they still believed they had been 20% faster. The clock and their own impression pointed in opposite directions.

The Copilot result is the obvious objection to this argument: give an agent a task with a defined result and a test, and the work goes faster. But someone chose that task, and someone decided that twelve passing tests meant done. METR adds that even experienced people can misjudge whether the tool helped. Judging the result takes evidence, not just the sense that the work moved quickly.

That judgement stays with the person directing the agent. A test shows that its own conditions passed. It cannot say whether those conditions cover the need, whether the software should exist, or whether anyone can rely on it.

What if the making is what you value?

The fear is real. In interviews with 25 writing professionals who had either adopted or resisted generative AI, one screenwriter said that losing the work itself “would make me miserable inside, because I really love these tasks that I do.” The study records different views of what made the job worth doing.

Photography stayed a craft after Kodak took over the developing. The craft moved into choosing what to photograph and which frames to keep. Evans, Frank and Klein made art out of exactly that.

When an agent does the making, choosing and judging can be practised as a craft, or treated as supervision left over at the end. The pilot study shows the risk of the second. Manual control held up, and the pilots who supervised less actively had more trouble with everything else.

If choosing and judging are what remain, are you practising them as a craft, or waiting to approve whatever comes back?
