Easington Colliery sits on the County Durham coast. In May 1993 the last shift came up. The pit had run since 1899; at peak it employed close to two and a half thousand men. The closure took the village's reason for existing with it.

The scene repeated, with local variations, dozens of times across the early 1990s -- and dozens of times before that across the American Rust Belt, dozens of times since across northern France and the German Ruhr. We have the hindsight now. The argument about whether the disruption was as bad as the residents said is over. The numbers settled it.

The reason to revisit those closures now is that the same structural argument is being made about the next reallocation. Alex Imas, an economist at Chicago Booth, argues in a piece called What will be scarce? that as AI cheapens the production of nearly everything else, demand will flow into what stays scarce: relational work, attention, presence, judgment. The relational sector grows in relative importance. Labour reallocates from what gets cheap to what stays expensive.

The math is clean. It is also, in its underlying structure, the same math that said manufacturing employment would reallocate smoothly into services in the 1980s. That is not a knock on the math. It is a knock on the gap between what the math says and what reallocation actually does to a place.

What reallocation actually looked like

In the abstract, the labour leaves the shrinking sector and arrives in the growing one. In the data -- looked at four decades on, with no need to argue about transition costs in the abstract -- the picture is clearer.

Detroit had 1.85 million people in 1950. It has about 633,000 today. The city declared the largest municipal bankruptcy in American history in 2013, with debts of eighteen to twenty billion dollars. The poverty rate has run around thirty percent through most of the last decade, against roughly twelve nationally. Downtown has been substantially recovered by professional services and real estate; the neighbourhoods further out have not. The city the standard story tells you was always coming back is, on the ground, two cities -- and one of them is still in the trough.

England's coalfields tell a similar story in a different register. In 1983 there were 175 working pits and 187,000 NUM members. By the early 1990s most pits were gone; by 2015 the last had closed. The Beatty and Fothergill State of the Coalfields series, run repeatedly over two decades, finds employment rates in those communities five to seven points below the national average, disability claim rates roughly double, and life expectancy about two years shorter. Some of the M1 corridor pit villages did recover, on the back of warehousing and logistics. Others, further out, did not.

Roubaix, on the Belgian border, was a textile city of 124,000 in 1900. It is 99,000 now. Around a third of working-age adults outside retirement are economically inactive -- against twelve to thirteen percent nationally. The poverty rate sits around forty-five percent, against fourteen percent nationally. It is, by the standard French statistical measure, the poorest commune above fifty thousand in metropolitan France. The Bassin minier further north peaked at 220,000 miners in 1947 and lost its last pit in 1990. The pithead landscapes were declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2012. They had become a memorial.

What the model leaves out

The reallocation story is not wrong. The labour did, eventually, mostly find other work. Aggregate national employment has been, by historical standards, fine. The model is right about the aggregate. It is silent about the distribution.

It is silent about two specific things. The first is a floor -- what happens, in income and in services, to the people and the places where the old work has gone away while the new work is elsewhere. The second is a bridge -- the institutional and physical infrastructure that gets a person, or a town, from the closed plant to whatever comes next. Neither falls out of the math. Both have to be supplied.

Where the floor and the bridge were absent, you got the Rust Belt, the English coalfields outside the M1 corridor, Roubaix, the Bassin minier. Aggregate employment was fine. The places themselves never came back.

The Ruhr

If those are the cases where transition was largely left to happen, the Ruhr is the case where it was largely organised.

The Ruhrgebiet -- the cluster of industrial cities running through North Rhine-Westphalia, Essen and Dortmund and Bochum and Duisburg and the smaller towns between -- was, at peak, the largest coal and steel region in Western Europe. It employed roughly 600,000 people in those two industries. It now employs fewer than 5,000. The last working hard-coal pit in Germany, Prosper-Haniel, closed in December 2018 with a public ceremony attended by the federal president. The shrinkage was not gentler than what happened in Detroit or Roubaix. By absolute numbers it was larger.

But the Ruhr today is not Detroit and not Roubaix. About five million people live in the metropolitan area. The unemployment rate is higher than the German national average but not catastrophically so. New universities -- Bochum, Duisburg-Essen, Dortmund -- were planted on top of the old industrial base in the 1960s and 70s and have grown into significant research institutions. The region was the European Capital of Culture in 2010. The slag heaps and gasworks have, in many cases, been remade as parks and visitor sites that draw weekend traffic from Düsseldorf and Cologne.

What did the Ruhr actually do? Between roughly 1968 and the early 2000s, the federal government, the state of North Rhine-Westphalia, and the European Regional Development Fund kept a floor under the region while building a bridge out of it. Subsidised employment in the closing industries was wound down on a multi-decade schedule, not a single-decade one. Significant landscape funds -- over six hundred and eighty million euros between 1991 and 2010, with eighty to ninety percent of that coming from federal and European sources -- paid for the conversion of the industrial sites into the parks and quarters that anchor the region today. The IBA Emscher Park initiative, running from 1989 to 1999, deliberately used architectural and ecological commissions in the most damaged Emscher valley as the visible signal that the old region was being remade rather than abandoned. The new universities had three decades to mature into the things that now employ people.

It is not a triumphant case. Wages in the Ruhr are below the German national average. Younger people leave for Munich and Hamburg. The political and social texture is recognisably post-industrial. What it isn't is collapsed. The region has a shape. It can be lived in. It is, in the proper sense of the word, a managed transition rather than a managed decline.

Pittsburgh in the United States is a partial American analog, with a steel collapse roughly parallel to the Ruhr's coal one. The city itself recovered, anchored by the University of Pittsburgh, Carnegie Mellon, and the UPMC hospital system. The mill towns surrounding it -- Homestead, Aliquippa, McKeesport -- did not. The Pittsburgh case shows, more than anything, that a floor and a bridge can be supplied in pieces; they were supplied for the city centre and not for the company towns ten miles up the river.

The two pieces are not subtle. They are visible in the documents and in the budgets. A floor: income support and public services that did not collapse alongside the industry, including pension and disability provisions for older workers who would not retrain, and a commitment to keep schools and hospitals running while population and tax base were under pressure. A bridge: deliberate investment in the institutions and physical plant that the next economy would need -- universities, transport, remediated land -- at a scale large enough to be economically meaningful, sustained for long enough that it could plausibly produce a result.

Both have to be present, and at scale, and for a long time. The Bassin minier got a partial floor and a thin bridge; it is, today, a UNESCO memorial. The English coalfields outside the M1 corridor got a partial floor and almost no bridge; the State of the Coalfields data still tracks the gap, two decades on. The Ruhr got both, sustained for forty years; it is, today, a place with a shape.

The political afterlife

The political economist Andrés Rodríguez-Pose, surveying the same set of regions in 2018, summarised what the politics of the un-bridged places now look like. "Persistent poverty, economic decay and lack of opportunities are at the root of considerable discontent in declining and lagging-behind areas the world over. Voting in such places," he wrote, "has become an opportunity to rebel against a system perceived to have forgotten and despised them." The pattern is visible from the English coalfields to the Bassin minier, from the Wallonian rust belt to eastern Ohio.

The architecture is not optional

The point is not that the AI transition will be the same as the industrial one. The technology is different. The timescales are likely shorter, which is worse, not better. The relational sector that Imas is right to point to is real, and it will, in aggregate, grow.

The point is that "the sector grows" is a statement about an aggregate. It is not a statement about a place, or a person, or a decade. The places that came through the last reallocation with a shape were the places that had a floor under them and a bridge in front of them, sustained at scale long enough to do their work. The places that didn't are still where they were left.

The architecture is not optional. It is the difference between the Ruhr and the Bassin minier. It is what the model assumes and does not provide.
