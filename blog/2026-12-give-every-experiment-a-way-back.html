Midway through the previews of The Divine Mrs S at Hampstead Theatre in 2024, Rachael Stirling walked off the stage, sat down with her colleagues in the stalls and burst into tears. “We still haven’t cracked it,” she said. The director, Anna Mackmin, told her she had. Then Mackmin turned to the journalist David Jays, who was watching, and said: “This is what previews are.”

A preview is a performance in front of a paying audience before the official opening. The play can still change. Lines can go, scenes can be cut, and if a change does not work, the company can go back to what it had the night before.

That arrangement changes what people are willing to try. Knowing you can return to a working version makes it easier to try the idea you are not sure of. The same is true when you direct an agent.

When the first night was the only night

Sarah Siddons, the 18th-century actor Stirling was playing, worked in a theatre without that space. Plays lived or died by their first performance. Ballet and opera still mostly work that way: the first public show is usually also press night.

The modern preview took shape on Broadway. In 1952 the musical Wish You Were Here had an onstage swimming pool too big to tour, so it played for nearly four weeks on Broadway before its opening night. In 1966 Cabaret became the first show to sell cheaper tickets for its previews. A gap opened between showing the work to the public and committing to it.

What the gap lets people try

The playwright David Eldridge says that cuts “only become fully clear in preview,” even after weeks of rehearsal. His play Beginning had a strong first preview, and the company still found it could cut a page and a half near the end. When the first audience for Middle judged a wife who leaves her marriage more harshly than he intended, he made cuts for the next preview. April De Angelis, who wrote The Divine Mrs S, described one night’s cuts plainly: “it was just blathering on.”

Each of those changes was made with an audience in the room and a few nights left to fix it. Rilwan Abiola Owokoniran, an actor about to start previews at the RSC, described what that does to his own choices: “Things can go wrong in previews, but if you buy into that, there’s a freedom – I can almost be braver in a preview.”

A mistake in a preview costs one evening. A mistake on press night goes into the reviews.

Keep the version that works

The freedom has a condition. In the same preview week, Mackmin gave Stirling a note: “You need to do a tiny percentage less in some places. It’s the smallest adjustment, it really is. Please do not unmake your performance.”

The changes in previews are made against something that already works. The company tries a cut, watches the audience, and keeps or drops it. The performance from the night before is the version they can return to. Without it, every experiment risks losing what was already good.

A good preview is not a guarantee

Previews do not settle whether the final version is right. Stirling had just played in Private Lives at the Donmar, where the company had made the play “sexier and naughtier and dirtier” and the preview audiences loved it. On press night, she said, “it was like we’d shat on a national treasure.”

A preview cannot promise that every change improves the work. It gives the people making it a chance to see how a choice lands, and to change it, before the commitment becomes harder to undo.

Save the working version first

Directing an agent can work the same way, if you set it up to. Before you ask for a large or uncertain change, ask the agent to save the version that works now, as a commit or a checkpoint. Then ask for the experiment. If it fails, ask the agent to go back to the saved version and try something else.

Without that saved version, an ambitious request puts the app you already have at risk, and it is reasonable to ask for less. With it, a failed idea costs the time it took to try, like a cut that did not work in one preview.

What would you try if you knew you could return to the last version that worked?
